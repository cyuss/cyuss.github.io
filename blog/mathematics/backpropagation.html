Backpropagation, How Neural Nets Actually Learn

20 Apr 2022

7 minutes read

A neural network makes a prediction, it is wrong, and a single number, the loss, says how wrong. But the network has millions of weights. Which ones are to blame, and by how much should each change ? Answering that for every weight, quickly, is the problem backpropagation solves. The idea is to assign blame backwards : the output layer knows its error directly, and it passes a share of the blame back to the layer before it, which passes it back again, all the way to the first weight. Mathematically this “blame-passing” is nothing but the chain rule of calculus, applied with ruthless efficiency. Together with gradient descent, which uses the blame to update the weights, backpropagation is the reason deep learning is possible at all.

1. The setup : a network is a chain of functions

A feedforward network is just a stack of layers, each taking the previous layer’s output, mixing it with weights, and bending it through a non-linearity $\sigma$. For layer $l$ :

\[\begin{equation} z^{l} = W^{l} a^{l-1} + b^{l}, \qquad a^{l} = \sigma(z^{l}), \label{eq:forward} \end{equation}\]

where $a^{l-1}$ is the input from below, $z^{l}$ is the pre-activation, and $a^{l}$ is the layer’s output. Running these equations from the input $a^0 = x$ up to the final layer is the forward pass : it produces the prediction and, compared against the target, the loss $L$.

In plain words : the forward pass is the network “thinking” , data flows in at the bottom and a prediction comes out the top. Backpropagation is the network “reflecting” on what went wrong, flowing backwards from top to bottom.

2. What we actually want

Gradient descent needs one thing : the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight, $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial W^{l}}$ and $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b^{l}}$, for all layers $l$. With millions of weights, computing each derivative separately would be hopeless. Backpropagation gets all of them in a single backward sweep, at roughly the cost of one forward pass.

3. The chain rule, ELI5

The chain rule tells you how a small nudge ripples through a composition of functions. If $L$ depends on $a$, which depends on $z$, which depends on $w$, then

\[\begin{equation} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\cdot\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}. \label{eq:chain} \end{equation}\]

In plain words : to know how much a weight deep in the network affects the final loss, you multiply together the little “how much does each step affect the next” factors along the path from that weight to the output. Blame is a product of local sensitivities, and backprop computes that product from the output end, reusing work as it goes.

4. The error signal and its backward flow

The clever bookkeeping trick is to define, for each layer, an error signal

\[\begin{equation} \delta^{l} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{l}}, \label{eq:delta} \end{equation}\]

how sensitive the loss is to that layer’s pre-activation. Two facts make everything fall into place. At the output layer, $\delta$ is immediate (the difference between prediction and target, shaped by the loss). And each layer’s error can be computed from the one above it :

\[\begin{equation} \delta^{l} = \big( (W^{l+1})^{\top}\, \delta^{l+1} \big) \odot \sigma'(z^{l}), \label{eq:backprop} \end{equation}\]

where $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication and $\sigma’$ the derivative of the activation. This is the “pass the blame back one layer” step : multiply by the transposed weights to route the error backwards, then scale by how active each neuron was. Once you have the $\delta$’s, the gradients you actually wanted are simply

\[\begin{equation} \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{l}} = \delta^{l} (a^{l-1})^{\top}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^{l}} = \delta^{l}. \label{eq:grads} \end{equation}\]

5. Why it is so efficient

The magic is reuse. A naive approach would recompute the long product $\eqref{eq:chain}$ from scratch for every weight. Backpropagation instead computes each $\delta^{l}$ once and reuses it for all the weights feeding that layer, exactly the “compute a subproblem once, store it, reuse it” spirit of memoization and dynamic programming. The result : the entire gradient over millions of weights costs about the same as a single forward pass. Without this, training large networks would be computationally impossible.

For a chain of layers, the blame reaching an early layer is a product of many factors $\eqref{eq:backprop}$. If those factors are consistently smaller than $1$, the product shrinks towards zero, the notorious vanishing gradient, and early layers barely learn. If they are larger than $1$, it explodes. This single observation explains a lot of modern design : the ReLU activation (whose derivative is exactly $1$ on the positive side), careful initialisation, and the residual connections of the Transformer, which give the gradient a clean shortcut straight back.

6. Conclusion

Backpropagation is often made to sound arcane, but it is just the chain rule with good bookkeeping : do a forward pass to see what the network predicted, then sweep backwards, handing each layer its error signal $\delta$ and reading off every gradient along the way. Feed those gradients to gradient descent and the weights inch towards something better ; repeat a few million times and a pile of random numbers becomes a model that can translate languages or recognise faces. The Universal Approximation Theorem promised a good network exists ; backpropagation and gradient descent are how we actually reach it. 1
